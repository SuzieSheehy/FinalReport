\chapter{Finite Size Scaling Effect}

Recalling Section \ref{sec:stats}, the finite size scaling law hypothesizes that the tail behaviour relates to the finite lattice size $L$ via a probability mass function of the form:
\[p(X=x; \tau, L, f, D) \propto x^{-\tau} f\left(\frac{x}{L^D}\right)\]
Here $f$ governs the tail behaviour of the distribution, i.e. for small values of $u$, $f(u) \approx 1$ and the distribution resembles a power law.

We first model the distribution separately for each lattice size $L$, obtain estimates, assess the fits, and compare these to pooled estimates obtained by relating the pmf form to the finite scaling function $f$ above.

\section{Exponentially Truncated Power Law}
We first consider the exponentially-truncated power law:
\begin{align*}
    p(X=x; \tau, c, x_{max}) &= \frac{1}{C_{\tau, c, x_{max}}} x^{-\tau} \exp\left( \frac{-x}{c} \right) \\
    \intertext{Where $C_{\tau, c, x_{max}}$ is the normalising constant:}
    C_{\tau, c, x_{max}} &= \sum_{k=1}^{x_{max}} k^{-\tau} \exp\left( -k / c \right) \\
\end{align*}

In the above pmf, $\tau$ and $x_{max}$ are, as before, the power law scaling coefficient and the support upper bound respectively, while the new parameter $c$ controls the location of the soft cutoff, where power law behaviour decays. The shape of the pmf under varying parameter values can be observed in Figure \ref{fig:exp_powerlaw_params}

The likelihood and log-likelihood are:
\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}; \tau, c, x_{max}) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{C_{\tau, c, x_{max}}} x_i^{-\tau} \exp\left( \frac{-x_i}{c} \right) \\
    \log \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}; \tau, c, x_{max}) &= -n \log C_{\tau, c, x_{max}} -\tau \sum_{i=1}^n \log x_i - \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \\
\end{align*}

Let $\mathbf{x}^j = (x_1^j, \cdots, x_n^j)$ be the set of simulated statistics from lattice size $L_j$. As before, for each $L$, we numerically optimise the log-likelihood function to obtain:
\[\hat{\tau}(L_j), \hat{c}(L_j) = \argmin_{\tau, c}\left\{ -\log \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}^j; \tau, c, x_{max}) \right\}\]

Set $\tau = \tau_s(L), x_{max} = s_{max}(L), c = c_s(L)$ for modelling the avalanche size, $\tau = \tau_a(L), x_{max} = a_{max}(L), c = c_a(L)$ for the avalanche area, and $\tau = \tau_d(L), x_{max} = d_{max}(L), c = c_d(L)$ for modelling the avalanche length.
Recall we fix $a_{max}(L) = L^2$, $d_{max}=2L$, and $s_{max}(L) = 10L^2$ as known.

The results in Table \ref{tab:powerlaw_exp_taus} are promising: for lattice sizes 40 and above, the powerlaw coefficient $\tau \approx 1$.
Further, in Figure \ref{fig:powerlaw_exp_taus} the log soft cutoffs $\log \hat{c}$ appear to scale linearly in $\log L$, a relationship that we will examine in more detail when computing the pooled estimators.

\section{Pooled Exponentially Truncated Power Law}

Consider again the Finite Size Scaling hypothesis:
\[p(X=x; \tau, L, f, D) \propto x^{-\tau} f\left(\frac{x}{L^D}\right)\]

In the previous section, we modelled 
\[p(X=x; \tau, L, c, D) \propto x^{-\tau} \exp\left(-\frac{x}{c}\right)\]

Therefore, if the chosen pmf is adequate to model the behaviour of the system, and the statistics obey finite size scaling, then we should observe a constant powerlaw coefficient $\tau_s(L) = \tau_s$, $\tau_a(L) = \tau_a$, $\tau_d(L) = \tau_d$ as well as the relationships $c_s(L) = k_s L^D_s$, $c_a(L) = k_a L ^D_a$, and $c_d(L) = k_d L ^D_d$ for some constants $k_s, k_a, k_d, D_s, D_a, D_d$. 

To assess whether this is plausible, consider the pooled likelihood. Let $\mathbf{L} = (L_1, L_2, \cdots, L_K)$ be the set of lattice sizes simulated, and let $\mathbf{x}^j = (x_1^j, \cdots, x_n^j)$ be the set of simulated statistics from lattice size $L_j$.

\[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{1}, \cdots \mathbf{x}_{K}; \mathbf{L}, \tau, k, D) = \prod_{j=1}^K \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{j}; \tau, c=k L_j ^{D}, x_{max} = x_{max}(L_j)) \]


The full dataset can be used to fit $\tau, k$, and $ D$ using maximum likelihood, and the fit can be compared against the individual fits. If the pmf is well-specified and the system obeys finite size scaling, the fits should not significantly degrade, and we should observe that $\log \hat{c} = \log \hat{k} + \hat{D} \log L$. In other words, the pooled fit should give a trendline on the graph of $\log \hat{c}$ vs $\log L$. While the estimates in Table \ref{tab:pooled_powerlaw_MLEs} appear reasonable, the results in Figure \ref{fig:pooled_unpooled_exp_comparison} show that the pooled parameters do not fully capture the behaviour of the pmf. In particular, $\hat{\tau}$ does not appear to be constant for either avalanche size nor area, and the fitted line on the graph of $\log \hat{c}$ vs $\log L$ is not a great fit. This suggests the estimates of $\hat{k}$ and $\hat{D}$ are smoothing over the excess error introduced by assuming a constant $\hat{\tau}$.

\section{Stretched Exponentially Truncated Power Law}

Based on the fits of the previous section, more flexibility is needed in the model to capture the steep tail decays, especially for the theoretically bounded avalanche area and length, and for small lattice sizes. We extend the exponentially truncated power law studied above with a stretching parameter $\beta$, which controls the steepness of the tail decay. We consider both the unpooled and pooled models.

The pmf is:
\begin{align*}
    p(X=x; \tau, c, \beta, x_{max}) &= \frac{1}{C_{\tau, c, \beta, x_{max}}} x^{-\tau} \exp\left( -\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)^\beta \right) \\
    \intertext{Where $C_{\tau, c, \beta, x_{max}}$ is the normalising constant:}
    C_{\tau, c, \beta, x_{max}} &= \sum_{k=1}^{x_{max}} k^{-\tau} \exp\left( -\left(k / c\right)^\beta \right) \\
\end{align*}

The shape of the pmf under varying parameter values can be observed in Figure \ref{fig:stretched_powerlaw_params}. Of particular interest is that $\beta$ can impose a rapid transition from linearity to decay in the log-log plot, which we especially observe in the avalanche area distribution.

The right plot shows the effect of increasing $\beta$ while keeping $\tau$, $x_{max}$, and $c$ constant, which results in steeper decay.The orange line is identical in all plots, corresponding to parameters $\tau = 0.5$, $x_{max} = 100$, $c = 50$, and $\beta = 1$.}
\label{fig:stretched_powerlaw_params}

The unpooled likelihood and log-likelihood are:
\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}; \tau, c, x_{max}, \beta) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{C_{\tau, c, \beta, x_{max}}} x_i^{-\tau} \exp\left( -\left(\frac{x_i}{c}\right)^\beta \right) \\
    \log \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}; \tau, c, \beta, x_{max}) &= -n \log C_{\tau, c, \beta, x_{max}} -\tau \sum_{i=1}^T \log x_i - \frac{1}{c^\beta}\sum_{i=1}^T x_i^\beta \\
\end{align*}

As before, we can extend this model under the finite size scaling hypothesis to be of the form
\[p(X=x; \tau, L, k, D, \beta) \propto x^{-\tau} \exp\left(-\left(\frac{x}{kL^D}\right)^\beta\right)\]

Where we assume $\tau(L) = \tau$, $\beta(L) = \beta$, as well as the relationship $c(L) = k L^D$,for some constants $k, D$ for each statistic. 

The pooled likelihood is:
\[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{1}, \cdots \mathbf{x}_{K}; \mathbf{L}, \tau, k, D, \beta) = \prod_{j=1}^K \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{j}; \tau, c=k L_j ^{D}, \beta, x_{max} = x_{max}(L_j)) \]

The unpooled fits in Figure \ref{fig:stretched_unpooled_fit} appear to model the pmfs very closely, capturing the strong decay associated with small $L$ and the area hard cutoff $L^2$. However, they still do not fully capture the behaviour of the avalanche $L_1$ length, which still has tail behaviour that is visually unexplained by the MLE fit.

The pooled fit reveals a very interesting difference between avalanche size and avalanche area. Examining Figure \ref{fig:stretched_pooled_unpooled_exp_comparison}, while avalanche area could plausibly have $\beta_a(L) = \beta_a$, $\beta_s$ has a clear $L$-dependence. As $L$ increases, the soft cutoff $c$ increases, but the steepness of the decay $\beta$ decreases. This makes sense, as repeated toppling of the same locations are interrupted by loss at the boundaries, but when the lattice size increases, more grains are retained in the interior for longer, allowing repeated toppling. This is not the case for area; for any $L$, the avalanche area cannot exceed $L^2$, and therefore a sharp decay is needed near the cutoff point. 

This distinction is clear in the plots of $\log \hat{c}$ vs $\log L$ in Figure \ref{fig:stretched_pooled_unpooled_exp_comparison} as well. While the avalanche area shows a well-fitted trendline for the pooled estimators, the avalanche size line does not appear to fit the estimates as well and is slightly offset from the trend in the unpooled estimators. This is because the estimates for $k$ and $D$ adapt to reduce the error of setting a fixed $\beta_s$. Because area can plausibly have a fixed $\beta_a$, this effect is minimised.

Comparing the unpooled MLE fits in Figure \ref{fig:stretched_unpooled_fit} with the pooled version in Figure \ref{fig:stretched_pooled_fit}, the pooled fit appears appropriate for avalanche area. Some worsening is expected, as the degrees of freedom for the fit drop, but it is not too noticeable. By contrast, the fits for avalanche size and $L_1$ length degrade visibly.

The above is consistent with previous work \cite{tebaldi1999multifractal}, which found that while avalanche area obeys finite size scaling, toppling size has multifractal scaling and is not consistent with finite size scaling. The fractal dimension $D_a \approx 2$ found here matches the estimation in \cite{tebaldi1999multifractal}, however the power law coefficient is lower than their estimate; we found $\tau_a \approx 1.02$ while they obtained $\tau_a = 1.2$. However, it is consistent with Bak et al.'s original paper \cite{btw1987intro}.

$L_1$ length differs from both size and area in that its estimated power law coefficient $\tau_d$ appears to have a clear $L$-dependence, as well as its estimated stretching parameter $\beta_d$. Surprisingly, the pooled fit for $\beta_d$ is larger than any individual fit. These signs indicate that the model may be mis-specified for length; even the manually-truncated data did not appear to scale fully as a power law. 

The scaling exponent estimates $\hat{D}_d \approx 1$ and $\hat{D}_s \approx 2.2$ seem reasonable based on what they represent; length is a 1D quantity, and size can exceed $L^2$ through repeated topplings. However, evidence suggests these results may not be fully representative, as discussed previously.
